2. How ACCA is likely to test these areasLink to this section

Section A is a 50-mark case mainly from syllabus A and B. This means TSR, NPS, sustainability, environmental costs, lifecycle costing, SHIP, SLAs and organisational change can appear together in a large case. Section C narrative commentary is expected mainly in one compulsory 25-mark Section B question on performance reporting.

APM does not reward isolated definitions. For each issue, use:

Evidence → technical analysis/calculation → objective or stakeholder impact → judgement → practical action

A strong paragraph answers:

  1. What does the scenario show?
  2. Why does it matter to performance or value creation?
  3. Is the measure/system fit for purpose?
  4. What should management do?
  5. What risk, behaviour or limitation remains?

PART I — STRATEGIC CHANGE AND PERFORMANCE SYSTEMSLink to this section

3. Changing structure, culture and strategy (A1i)Link to this section

The new learning outcome asks you to assess how changing organisational structure, culture and strategy affects processes, systems and people. It is not a request for generic change-management theory. Start with the proposed change and trace its performance-management consequences.

3.1 StructureLink to this section

Possible structural changes include centralisation/decentralisation, functional to divisional/process structure, outsourcing, shared services, matrix working, strategic alliances and virtual/network structures.

Effect on processesLink to this section

  • Decision rights, approvals and accountability change.
  • Cross-functional processes may replace departmental hand-offs.
  • Standardisation may improve efficiency but reduce local responsiveness.
  • Outsourcing creates interfaces, contracts and supplier-monitoring processes.
  • Decentralisation speeds local decisions but can duplicate activities.

Effect on systemsLink to this section

  • Reporting lines and responsibility centres must match the new structure.
  • KPIs, budgets and transfer prices may require redesign.
  • Integrated information may be needed across divisions or partners.
  • Access rights, master data and reporting frequency must change.
  • Existing reports can become misleading if old cost centres or comparators remain.

Effect on peopleLink to this section

  • Roles, skills, workload and career paths change.
  • Managers may gain or lose autonomy and resist the change.
  • Accountability without controllability demotivates employees.
  • Training and communication are needed before new targets are enforced.
  • Redundancy, redeployment and perceived unfairness can damage morale.

3.2 CultureLink to this section

Culture is the shared assumptions, norms and behaviours that influence how work is done.

A collaborative culture supports shared KPIs, knowledge exchange and process improvement. A blame culture encourages concealment, gaming and risk avoidance. An innovative culture needs tolerance of controlled failure and measures of learning, not only short-term profit. A strong cost culture may support efficiency but harm quality, innovation or customer experience.

When strategy or systems conflict with culture, formal measures may not change actual behaviour. Management should align leadership behaviour, recruitment, training, rewards, controls and narratives with the desired culture.

3.3 StrategyLink to this section

A new strategy changes critical success factors (CSFs), which should change objectives, KPIs, targets, information and rewards.

Examples:

  • Cost leadership: unit cost, productivity, capacity use and process time—but safeguard quality and innovation.
  • Differentiation: quality, innovation, brand, service and customer retention—not only volume.
  • Sustainability: lifecycle emissions, waste, supplier compliance and long-term resource resilience.
  • Digital growth: conversion, retention, availability and customer experience alongside profitability.

3.4 Exam framework: SPARCLink to this section

  • S — Strategic coherence: does the proposed structure/culture support the strategy?
  • P — Processes: what workflows, controls and decision rights change?
  • A — Architecture of information: which systems, reports and data are required?
  • R — Roles and rewards: who owns performance, and are measures controllable?
  • C — Change risk: skills, resistance, disruption, cost and review.

3.5 Likely requirementLink to this section

Assess how the proposed move from regional divisions to centralised shared services will affect the organisation's processes, systems and people.

Do not merely list advantages of centralisation. Apply central processing savings, loss of regional knowledge, new service standards, ERP integration, changed responsibility, transition disruption and employee resistance to the scenario.

PART II — TOTAL SHAREHOLDER RETURNLink to this section

4. Total shareholder return (TSR) (A3biv)Link to this section

TSR measures the total return earned by a shareholder from both capital gain/loss and dividends over a period.

4.1 FormulaLink to this section

TSR = (Closing share price − Opening share price + Dividend per share) / Opening share price × 100%

If dividends are reinvested or there are several periods, use the information/method required by the question. Unless instructed otherwise, the one-period formula above is the standard approach.

4.2 Worked exampleLink to this section

Opening share price = $4.80
Closing share price = $5.25
Dividend per share = $0.18

Capital gain = $5.25 − $4.80 = $0.45
Total shareholder return per share = $0.45 + $0.18 = $0.63

TSR = $0.63 / $4.80 × 100 = 13.125%, approximately 13.1%

Separate the components if useful:

  • Capital gain yield = $0.45 / $4.80 = 9.4%
  • Dividend yield = $0.18 / $4.80 = 3.8%
  • Total = 13.1%

4.3 Why TSR is usefulLink to this section

  • Directly reflects return actually available to shareholders.
  • Combines income and capital growth; dividend yield alone is incomplete.
  • Market based and forward looking because share price reflects expectations.
  • Allows comparison with competitors, an index and shareholder required return.
  • Connects to value-based management better than accounting profit alone.
  • Useful for long-term executive rewards when measured relatively and over several years.

4.4 LimitationsLink to this section

  • Share price is affected by economy, interest rates, market sentiment and sector events outside management control.
  • A short measurement window may be volatile and encourage timing manipulation.
  • Market value can overreact or underreact and may not represent underlying performance.
  • A private or unlisted company has no observable market price.
  • TSR says that value changed but not why; operational drivers remain necessary.
  • High dividends can improve current TSR while reducing funds for positive-NPV investment.
  • Comparison is misleading unless period, currency, dividend treatment and risk are consistent.
  • Absolute TSR can be positive while underperforming the market; relative TSR may be more informative.

4.5 TSR versus EPSLink to this section

EPS TSR
Accounting profit per ordinary share Market return to shareholders
Historical and affected by accounting policy Includes market expectations and dividends
Can rise through share buybacks without operating improvement Reflects price response but also external market factors
Does not charge for equity capital Can be compared with required return/market benchmark
Useful indicator of profitability Better direct indicator of shareholder wealth

Do not say TSR replaces EPS. Use a balanced package: TSR for shareholder outcome; EPS/profitability for accounting performance; cash flow, EVA/RI and operational drivers for explanation.

4.6 How ACCA may ask itLink to this section

  • Calculate TSR and interpret movement against a comparator.
  • Evaluate TSR as a board or executive-reward measure.
  • Explain why profit/EPS growth did not create shareholder value.
  • Recommend a balanced set of shareholder measures.

4.7 High-scoring interpretationLink to this section

Weak: “TSR is 13.1%, so performance is good.”

Strong: “TSR of 13.1% is positive, mainly from a 9.4% capital gain. It cannot be judged good without the sector return and shareholders' required return. If the market rose 18%, the company destroyed relative value despite its positive absolute TSR. Management should therefore compare multi-year relative TSR and explain the operational value drivers rather than using one year's return alone.”

PART III — NET PROMOTER SCORELink to this section

5. Net promoter score (NPS) (A4b)Link to this section

NPS is based on asking customers how likely they are to recommend the organisation/product, commonly on a scale from 0 to 10.

  • Promoters: 9–10
  • Passives: 7–8
  • Detractors: 0–6

5.1 FormulaLink to this section

NPS = Percentage of promoters − Percentage of detractors

Passives are included in total respondents when calculating percentages but are not subtracted or added directly. NPS ranges from −100 to +100; it is not itself a percentage despite being calculated from percentages.

5.2 Worked exampleLink to this section

From 500 responses:

  • Promoters = 280 = 56%
  • Passives = 110 = 22%
  • Detractors = 110 = 22%

NPS = 56 − 22 = +34

Do not calculate 280 − 110 or divide only by promoters plus detractors.

5.3 BenefitsLink to this section

  • Simple and easily communicated.
  • Provides a consistent trend measure of customer advocacy.
  • Can be benchmarked by branch, channel, product and competitor where methodology is comparable.
  • Encourages attention to customer experience and retention.
  • May be a leading indicator of repeat business, referrals and revenue.

5.4 Limitations and scepticismLink to this section

  • Recommendation intention may not become actual recommendation or purchase.
  • Response rate and sampling can create bias; dissatisfied or highly engaged customers may dominate.
  • Cultural differences affect scoring behaviour.
  • A single number hides the reason for the score and distribution of responses.
  • Different collection points (immediately after service vs later) are not comparable.
  • Staff can game it by asking only satisfied customers or pressuring them for high scores.
  • Small branch samples can create volatile rankings.
  • NPS may not correlate with profitability: promoters may be low-margin customers.
  • A rising aggregate NPS may hide deterioration in a strategically important segment.

5.5 How to use NPS properlyLink to this section

  • Report response number/rate, method and confidence alongside the score.
  • Track trend using consistent questions and timing.
  • Segment by customer type, product, channel and region.
  • Analyse free-text reasons and complaint data.
  • Link NPS with retention, repeat purchase, customer lifetime value and contribution.
  • Do not reward employees on NPS alone; combine with quality and behavioural safeguards.

5.6 Likely requirementLink to this section

Evaluate whether the proposed NPS is a suitable KPI for customer loyalty.

Apply its relevance to the strategy, collection method, sample, controllability, gaming risk and whether it is connected to actual retention/profit.

PART IV — SUSTAINABILITY, REPORTING AND ENVIRONMENTAL COSTSLink to this section

6. Sustainability in strategic performance management (A5a–b)Link to this section

Sustainability means meeting present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs. For APM, it is a strategic performance issue, not a public-relations appendix.

6.1 The 3Ps / triple bottom lineLink to this section

  • People: employees, customers, suppliers and communities; health and safety, fair work, diversity, access and social impact.
  • Planet: energy, water, materials, emissions, biodiversity, waste and pollution.
  • Profit/prosperity: financial resilience and wider economic value, including jobs, taxes, innovation and infrastructure.

The 3Ps interact. Lower material/energy use can reduce both environmental harm and cost. Better safety can reduce absence, claims and disruption. Poor supplier labour standards can damage reputation and sales.

6.2 Translating sustainability into performance managementLink to this section

Use a clear hierarchy:

Mission/values → strategic sustainability goal → objective → CSF → KPI → target → initiative → owner → review

Example:

  • Goal: reduce environmental impact while growing profitably.
  • Objective: reduce lifecycle carbon intensity by 30% by 20X8.
  • CSFs: energy-efficient production and lower-carbon supply chain.
  • KPIs: tonnes CO2e per unit; renewable energy percentage; supplier emissions coverage.
  • Target: defined baseline, boundary and date.
  • Initiatives: equipment upgrade, design change and supplier programme.
  • Controls: consistent methodology, assurance and corrective action.

6.3 Sustainability and the Balanced ScorecardLink to this section

Sustainability can be embedded within existing perspectives rather than treated as a disconnected fifth perspective:

  • Financial: energy cost savings, carbon cost exposure, sustainable-product margin.
  • Customer: demand for sustainable products, trust and complaints.
  • Internal process: waste yield, emissions, water, responsible sourcing.
  • Learning and growth: sustainability training, innovation and culture.

A separate sustainability perspective may highlight strategic importance but can create a silo. Judge which design better integrates sustainability into daily decisions.

7. Sustainability targets and KPIsLink to this section

Environmental indicatorsLink to this section

  • Absolute greenhouse gas emissions and emissions per unit/revenue.
  • Energy consumption and percentage renewable.
  • Material yield, recycled input and product recyclability.
  • Water withdrawal, consumption and recycling in water-stressed locations.
  • Waste by type, waste intensity and landfill diversion.
  • Supplier sustainability compliance and supply-chain miles.

Social indicatorsLink to this section

  • Lost-time injury frequency/severity.
  • Employee turnover, absence, engagement and training.
  • Pay equity, diversity and promotion rates.
  • Supplier labour audits and corrective-action closure.
  • Customer access, affordability, complaints and safety.
  • Community investment and measurable outcomes.

Economic indicatorsLink to this section

  • Sustainable-product revenue/margin.
  • Tax and jobs supported.
  • Long-term investment and R&D.
  • Value distributed to stakeholders.
  • Supply-chain resilience and local sourcing where relevant.

Evaluate KPI quality using BOUNDLink to this section

  • B — Boundary: which entity, sites, activities and supply-chain stages?
  • O — Objective linkage: does it measure the strategic sustainability goal?
  • U — Units and normalisation: absolute amount, intensity or both?
  • N — Neutrality: balanced good/bad news; no cherry-picking or greenwashing?
  • D — Data reliability: definitions, source, estimates, consistency and assurance?

Absolute and intensity measures should often be presented together. Emissions per unit may fall while total emissions rise due to growth.

8. Sustainability and integrated reporting (A5b)Link to this section

Integrated reporting (IR) explains how an organisation's strategy, governance, performance and prospects create value over the short, medium and long term. It promotes integrated thinking rather than simply combining an annual report with a sustainability report.

8.1 Six capitalsLink to this section

  1. Financial
  2. Manufactured
  3. Intellectual
  4. Human
  5. Social and relationship
  6. Natural

The capitals are inputs to the business model, are transformed by activities and may increase, decrease or be converted. Value is not only financial value for shareholders; activities affect stakeholders and future access to resources.

8.2 Core integrated-report contentLink to this section

  • Organisational overview and external environment.
  • Governance.
  • Business model: inputs, activities, outputs and outcomes.
  • Risks and opportunities.
  • Strategy and resource allocation.
  • Performance.
  • Outlook.
  • Basis of preparation and presentation.

8.3 Key principlesLink to this section

  • Strategic focus and future orientation.
  • Connectivity of information.
  • Stakeholder relationships.
  • Materiality.
  • Conciseness.
  • Reliability and completeness.
  • Consistency and comparability.

8.4 BenefitsLink to this section

  • Connects strategy, risks, resources, non-financial drivers and financial outcomes.
  • Encourages long-term decisions and reduces functional silos.
  • Improves board understanding of trade-offs among capitals.
  • Helps investors understand future value drivers not visible in financial statements.
  • Can improve internal data, accountability and resource allocation.

8.5 Limitations and risksLink to this section

  • Judgement over materiality and boundaries reduces comparability.
  • Non-financial data may be estimated, inconsistent or weakly controlled.
  • Cost of systems, expertise and assurance.
  • Commercial sensitivity and report overload.
  • Management may use selective positive stories: greenwashing or impression management.
  • Quantifying some capital/outcomes is difficult and monetisation can create false precision.
  • Producing a report without integrated decision-making is mere compliance.

8.6 How ACCA may ask sustainability reportingLink to this section

  • Evaluate whether current sustainability measures are fit for strategic objectives.
  • Recommend KPIs and targets from scenario impacts.
  • Assess the value/limitations of integrated reporting.
  • Identify greenwashing, inconsistent boundaries or missing bad news.
  • Link sustainability initiatives to cost, risk, customers and long-term value.

9. Environmental cost categories (A5c)Link to this section

The syllabus names four categories:

9.1 Conventional costsLink to this section

Normal raw material, energy, water, labour and equipment costs that have environmental relevance.

Examples: energy used by inefficient machinery; material that becomes scrap; water purchased and treated.

These costs are visible in accounts but may not be recognised as environmental. Reducing waste can reduce them immediately.

9.2 Hidden costsLink to this section

Environment-related costs lost within general overhead or another account, so managers cannot see the activity/product causing them.

Examples: environmental monitoring, permits, waste administration, training, compliance staff, reporting and handling.

Use ABC, separate accounts and process analysis to reveal and allocate them appropriately.

9.3 Contingent costsLink to this section

Potential future costs dependent on uncertain events.

Examples: cleanup/remediation, fines, litigation, decommissioning, restoration, carbon-price exposure and product take-back.

Assess probability, magnitude, timing, scenarios and risk. A cost not recognised as an accounting provision can still be strategically important.

9.4 Reputational/image and relationship costsLink to this section

Costs/benefits arising from stakeholder perceptions and relationships, often intangible and difficult to quantify.

Examples: lost sales, boycott, higher recruitment cost, financing/insurance difficulty, brand recovery, community engagement and sustainability reporting.

Do not assume intangible means immaterial. Use indicators such as customer loss, media sentiment, employee turnover and cost of capital, while acknowledging attribution uncertainty.

9.5 Exam calculation and interpretationLink to this section

If given environmental costs, classify them, total by category, calculate percentage of revenue/operating cost and compare trends. Then explain action.

Example:

Category 20X4 20X5 Change
Conventional energy/material waste $800k $960k +20%
Hidden compliance/monitoring $180k $270k +50%
Expected contingent remediation $100k $300k +200%
Reputational response $60k $150k +150%

Do not conclude only that total cost increased. Investigate whether output rose, identify cost drivers, distinguish prevention investment from failure cost, and prioritise the contingent/reputational escalation because it may signal control failure.

10. Environmental management accounting techniques (A5d–e)Link to this section

10.1 Lifecycle costingLink to this section

Include costs and environmental consequences from research/design through production, distribution, use, after-sales and disposal (“cradle to grave”; where circularity applies, consider reuse/recovery).

Use it to compare design alternatives and avoid shifting cost or harm to another lifecycle stage.

10.2 Input–output analysisLink to this section

Physical mass/energy balance: inputs must become saleable output, by-product, stored material, emissions or waste.

Material loss = input − useful output − saleable/recoverable by-product

Value wasted material at purchase cost and add processing/disposal cost where relevant. The method exposes both physical inefficiency and hidden cost.

Example: 10,000 kg input; 8,400 kg product; 600 kg saleable scrap; 1,000 kg waste. Material yield = 84%. Waste rate = 10%. Management should trace the process cause and assess whether design, supplier quality or process control can improve yield.

10.3 Activity-based costing (ABC)Link to this section

Identify environmental activities, cost pools and drivers; allocate costs to the products/processes that cause them.

Examples:

  • Waste handling driven by kg of hazardous waste.
  • Emissions testing driven by production batches.
  • Water treatment driven by litres/contaminant load.
  • Supplier audits driven by number/risk of suppliers.

ABC avoids cross-subsidisation and improves product, pricing, process and discontinuation decisions. It costs more to operate and driver relationships may still be imperfect.

10.4 Interpretation and actionLink to this section

For every calculation answer:

  • Which product/process causes the cost?
  • Is the issue prevention, detection or failure?
  • What operational change is available?
  • What cost, quality, customer or sustainability trade-off follows?
  • Which KPI will confirm improvement?

PART V — LIFECYCLE COSTING FOR PERFORMANCE OPTIMISATIONLink to this section

11. Lifecycle costing (B3diii)Link to this section

Lifecycle costing now explicitly appears among techniques used to optimise performance. It includes all costs over a product/service life, not just current manufacturing cost.

11.1 Lifecycle stages and costsLink to this section

  1. Research, market analysis and concept.
  2. Design, development and testing.
  3. Production/service delivery.
  4. Marketing, distribution and customer support.
  5. Warranty, maintenance and returns.
  6. Withdrawal, decommissioning, disposal and remediation.

11.2 FormulaLink to this section

Lifecycle cost per unit = Total whole-life costs / Expected lifetime units

Lifecycle profit = Total lifetime revenue − Total lifetime costs

Use discounting/NPV when timing is material and the question provides the rate or requires it.

11.3 Committed versus incurred costsLink to this section

Costs are incurred when resources are used, but many are committed by earlier decisions. Design commonly locks in most later material, production, warranty and disposal cost. Therefore the greatest optimisation opportunity is often before production.

11.4 Worked exampleLink to this section

Expected volume: 40,000 units
Design/R&D: $1,200,000
Production: $42 per unit
Marketing/distribution: $9 per unit
Warranty: $240,000
End-of-life: $160,000

Total cost = $1,200,000 + ($42 × 40,000) + ($9 × 40,000) + $240,000 + $160,000
= $3,640,000

Lifecycle cost per unit = $3,640,000 / 40,000 = $91

At selling price $105, lifecycle profit per unit = $14 and total profit = $560,000.

If extra design spending of $200,000 cuts production cost by $7 per unit, saving = $280,000; net lifecycle saving = $80,000 before time value and risk. Recommend it only if quality, timing and demand assumptions remain acceptable.

11.5 BenefitsLink to this section

  • Prevents apparently profitable products hiding pre/post-production costs.
  • Encourages early design intervention and cross-functional decisions.
  • Aligns pricing and target costing with long-term profitability.
  • Exposes warranty, service, environmental and withdrawal consequences.
  • Supports product mix, make/buy and discontinuation decisions.

11.6 LimitationsLink to this section

  • Long-term volume, price and cost estimates are uncertain.
  • Shared costs and end-of-life obligations are difficult to allocate.
  • Product changes and inflation reduce forecast reliability.
  • Detailed modelling can be expensive.
  • Managers rewarded annually may resist upfront cost for future benefits.
  • Average lifecycle cost can hide customer/product variants.

11.7 Likely requirementLink to this section

  • Calculate lifecycle cost/profit and advise whether to launch/redesign.
  • Evaluate lifecycle costing versus annual/conventional costing.
  • Recommend actions to optimise cost across stages.
  • Integrate environmental effects, target costing and NPV.

PART VI — SERVICE BUSINESSES AND SHIPLink to this section

12. SHIP characteristics (B4a)Link to this section

SHIP summarises characteristics that make services difficult to measure and manage:

12.1 SimultaneityLink to this section

Production and consumption often occur together, with customer participation.

Implications:

  • Limited opportunity to inspect before delivery.
  • Employee/customer interaction strongly affects quality.
  • Failure is visible immediately and service recovery matters.
  • Demand and staffing must be coordinated in real time.

Measures: first-time resolution, waiting time, service recovery, customer satisfaction, abandonment and employee competence.

12.2 HeterogeneityLink to this section

Service quality varies by employee, customer, time and location.

Implications:

  • Standardisation and comparison are difficult.
  • Customer needs legitimately produce variation.
  • Training, scripts and systems can improve consistency but excessive standardisation reduces personalisation.

Measures: variation/range, error/rework, complaints by employee/branch, mystery shopping, quality audits and satisfaction distribution—not only average.

12.3 IntangibilityLink to this section

Services cannot usually be touched, stored or inspected like physical goods; customers assess experience and outcomes.

Implications:

  • Quality and output may be subjective.
  • Harder to communicate value and compare providers.
  • Brand, trust and physical evidence become important.
  • Input/activity measures may be easier than true outcomes but can mislead.

Measures: outcomes, customer-reported experience, retention, referrals/NPS, professional quality review and complaint resolution.

12.4 PerishabilityLink to this section

Unused service capacity cannot be inventoried for later sale.

Implications:

  • Empty hotel rooms/airline seats/consultant hours are lost revenue.
  • Peaks create queues and quality pressure; troughs create idle cost.
  • Capacity, appointments, flexible staffing, reservations and pricing are central.

Measures: capacity utilisation, occupancy/load factor, no-show rate, waiting time, revenue per available capacity unit and demand forecast accuracy.

12.5 APM evaluation warningLink to this section

Never write four definitions only. For each characteristic explain:

scenario feature → measurement/control problem → relevant measure/action → trade-off

Example: raising hospital bed utilisation may improve efficiency but reduce emergency resilience and effectiveness. Measure utilisation with waiting time, cancellation, outcome and safety.

12.6 Link to Fitzgerald and MoonLink to this section

Service performance can be evaluated through:

  • Results: financial performance and competitiveness.
  • Determinants: quality, flexibility, resource utilisation and innovation.
  • Standards: ownership, achievability and equity.
  • Rewards: clarity, motivation and controllability.

SHIP explains why the determinants and well-designed standards are particularly important in services.

PART VII — SERVICE LEVEL AGREEMENTSLink to this section

13. Service level agreements (B4i)Link to this section

An SLA is an agreed specification of service, responsibilities, performance standards, reporting and remedies between provider and customer/internal parties. It converts expectations into controllable, measurable commitments.

13.1 Essential contentLink to this section

  1. Parties, purpose, scope and excluded services.
  2. Clear service definitions and operating hours.
  3. Roles, responsibilities, dependencies and customer obligations.
  4. KPIs with exact definitions, data source and calculation.
  5. Target/service levels, tolerance and priority classes.
  6. Measurement period, reporting frequency and access to evidence.
  7. Incident logging, response, resolution and escalation.
  8. Availability, continuity, security, privacy and recovery requirements.
  9. Change-control process.
  10. Charges, incentives, service credits/penalties and limits.
  11. Governance meetings, dispute resolution and audit rights.
  12. Review, renewal, termination and exit/data-transfer provisions.
  13. Ethical, legal and sustainability standards where relevant.

13.2 Good SLA measuresLink to this section

  • Availability = available service time / agreed service time.
  • On-time delivery percentage.
  • Response and resolution time by severity.
  • First-contact resolution.
  • Defect/rejection or rework rate.
  • Customer satisfaction/complaint rate.
  • Recovery time and data-loss tolerance.

Define exclusions carefully. A provider could game availability by classifying outages as planned maintenance or excluding peak periods.

13.3 BenefitsLink to this section

  • Clarifies expectations and accountability.
  • Enables objective monitoring of an external/internal service.
  • Aligns capacity and service priorities with customer need.
  • Provides evidence and escalation when performance fails.
  • Supports supplier comparison, continuous improvement and risk control.

13.4 Problems and behavioural risksLink to this section

  • What is measurable may replace what is valuable.
  • Provider may meet targets while customer outcomes remain poor.
  • Aggressive penalties create concealment, defensive behaviour or risk premium.
  • Too many KPIs increase cost and dilute priorities.
  • Responsibility disputes occur where several partners affect one outcome.
  • Provider may optimise the average while severe cases fail.
  • Fixed SLA becomes obsolete as strategy, demand or technology changes.
  • Monitoring data may be controlled by the provider and require audit.

13.5 Implementation and managementLink to this section

  • Consult users and process owners; identify CSFs and risks.
  • Establish baseline and realistic targets.
  • Use a small balanced set of outcome, quality, time and risk measures.
  • Assign owners and agree data definitions before signing.
  • Pilot where possible and train both parties.
  • Use dashboards, exception reports and regular service reviews.
  • Perform root-cause analysis, not only impose penalties.
  • Update targets and scope through controlled review.
  • Maintain contingency/exit plans for critical outsourced services.

13.6 Internal SLA versus external contractLink to this section

An internal SLA may not be legally enforceable but clarifies shared-service accountability and chargeback. An external SLA sits within a contract and should include commercial/legal remedies. In both, partnership and shared objectives often create better long-term performance than punishment alone.

13.7 Likely requirementLink to this section

Advise on the content and management of an SLA for an outsourced logistics/cloud/customer-service provider.

Select scenario-specific standards. Do not reproduce a generic list without linking the provider failure to customers, strategy, cost and risk.

PART VIII — NARRATIVE PERFORMANCE COMMENTARYLink to this section

14. How narrative commentary can mislead (C1d)Link to this section

Narrative can mislead even where every number is technically correct.

14.1 Common manipulation methodsLink to this section

  • Selective emphasis on good news and omission/burying of bad news.
  • Vague claims: “strong”, “significant”, “industry-leading” without evidence.
  • Claiming causation from correlation.
  • Attributing bad outcomes to external factors but good outcomes to management.
  • Changing definitions, periods or comparators without disclosure.
  • Using percentage changes without absolute amount/base.
  • Discussing revenue growth while ignoring margin/cash decline.
  • Cherry-picking one favourable segment or start date.
  • Presenting adjusted measures without reconciliation.
  • Using averages that hide dispersion/outliers.
  • Describing targets as achieved despite lowered/revised targets.
  • Excessive volume/technical language that obscures key issues.
  • Greenwashing: broad sustainability claims without boundaries, data or balanced outcomes.
  • False certainty, unsupported forecasts and omission of assumptions.

14.2 Professional scepticism checklist: CLEARLink to this section

  • C — Comparators: budget, prior period, benchmark and strategy consistent?
  • L — Language: neutral, precise and supported rather than promotional?
  • E — Evidence: calculations, sources, sample and definitions reliable?
  • A — All material information: adverse trends, risk and uncertainty included?
  • R — Reasons and response: genuine causes, implications and actions explained?

15. Preparing useful narrative commentary (C1e)Link to this section

A useful commentary converts data into insight and action. It does not narrate every number.

15.1 Structure: WHAT–WHY–SO WHAT–NOW WHATLink to this section

  1. What: overall result and material variance/trend, quantified.
  2. Why: evidence-based drivers, separating volume, price, mix, efficiency and external factors where possible.
  3. So what: consequence for objective, stakeholder, risk and future performance.
  4. Now what: specific corrective action, owner/priority and measure of success.

15.2 ExampleLink to this section

Data: revenue +8%; operating profit −6%; complaints +35%; on-time delivery down from 94% to 82%.

Weak commentary:

Revenue performed strongly, although profit was slightly below last year. Management continues to focus on customers.

Strong commentary:

Revenue increased 8%, but operating profit fell 6%, indicating that growth has not converted into value. The deterioration coincided with on-time delivery falling 12 percentage points to 82% and complaints rising 35%, suggesting that capacity or fulfilment problems are increasing recovery and logistics costs and may threaten retention. Management should analyse margin and complaints by channel, address the delivery bottleneck, and track on-time delivery, repeat purchase and contribution monthly. External cost inflation should be quantified separately before it is accepted as the explanation.

The strong version selects material data, links indicators, avoids claiming proven causation, identifies implications and recommends action.

15.3 Good commentary qualitiesLink to this section

  • Concise, objective and tailored to the user.
  • Starts with mission/objectives and material overall picture.
  • Quantifies rather than uses vague adjectives.
  • Uses like-for-like comparators and explains definition changes.
  • Integrates financial and non-financial leading/lagging indicators.
  • Balanced: successes, failures, uncertainty and risk.
  • Explains cause only to the extent evidence supports it.
  • Forward-looking with practical recommendations.

15.4 Likely Section B questionLink to this section

You may receive a draft management report, graphs and a director's commentary and be asked to:

  • advise how commentary misrepresents performance;
  • calculate missing trends/margins;
  • prepare replacement commentary;
  • demonstrate scepticism and commercial acumen.

Read the narrative against the data line by line. Identify what was said, what was omitted and what users could wrongly conclude.

18.1 PAIR paragraphLink to this section

  • Point: technical issue or finding.
  • Apply: scenario evidence/calculation.
  • Impact: objective, stakeholder, risk or value.
  • Recommend: action, safeguard or judgement.

18.2 “Evaluate a measure”Link to this section

  1. Purpose/objective and user.
  2. Correct calculation/definition.
  3. What insight it provides.
  4. Data, comparator and controllability limitations.
  5. Behavioural consequences.
  6. Complementary measures.
  7. Overall judgement and refinement.

For each KPI state:

  • exact definition/formula;
  • linked CSF/objective;
  • why it predicts or measures success;

19. Mini integrated caseLink to this section

ScenarioLink to this section

EcoStay is a listed hotel group pursuing premium sustainable hospitality. Revenue rose 10%, EPS rose 4%, but its share price fell from $6.00 to $5.55 after a $0.18 dividend. Guest NPS is reported as +48 from an app survey completed by 12% of guests; reception staff invite selected guests to participate. Room occupancy is 91%, while complaints about queues and cleanliness rose 30%. EcoStay claims it is “carbon neutral” because it buys offsets, but excludes outsourced laundry and guest energy use. It plans to outsource housekeeping under an SLA measuring only rooms cleaned per employee-hour.

Model pointsLink to this section

TSRLink to this section

TSR = ($5.55 − $6.00 + $0.18) / $6.00 = −4.5%. EPS growth did not translate into shareholder return. Compare with sector/market and assess whether investors doubt service quality or sustainability credibility.

NPSLink to this section

+48 appears positive, but 12% response and selective invitation create bias. Analyse all guest segments and collection points; link to repeat booking, complaints and contribution. NPS conflicts with rising complaints, requiring investigation.

SHIP and occupancyLink to this section

Perishable room capacity makes occupancy important, but 91% may strain simultaneous service delivery. Heterogeneity between housekeeping teams and intangible quality require cleanliness audits, room-ready time, complaints and repeat booking. Maximising occupancy alone may harm premium positioning.

SustainabilityLink to this section

The carbon-neutral claim has an incomplete boundary and may be greenwashing. Measure absolute and per-guest-night emissions, include material value-chain sources, prioritise actual reduction before offsets, explain methodology and obtain assurance.

SLALink to this section

Rooms per employee-hour rewards speed, not cleanliness or guest outcome. Add room inspection pass rate, room-ready time, complaints, rework, staff safety, sustainability standards and escalation. Agree data, responsibilities and review; avoid penalties that encourage concealment.

CommentaryLink to this section

A balanced narrative should report revenue/EPS growth alongside negative TSR, complaints and measurement limitations, explain uncertainty and state corrective actions.

20. Common mistakes that lose marksLink to this section

  1. Calling every reordered outcome “new”.
  2. Calculating TSR without dividends or using closing price as denominator.
  3. Judging TSR without a market/sector/required-return comparator.
  4. Treating NPS as a percentage or including passives in the subtraction.
  5. Recommending NPS without checking sample and gaming.
  6. Listing sustainability KPIs unrelated to the scenario's material impacts.
  7. Reporting intensity only and hiding rising absolute impact.
  8. Confusing sustainability reporting with integrated thinking.
  9. Defining environmental cost categories without analysing action.
  10. Ignoring pre- and post-production lifecycle costs.
  11. Defining SHIP without explaining measurement consequences.
  12. Writing a generic SLA checklist without scenario standards.
  13. Repeating narrative data instead of creating insight.
  14. Accepting management's causal explanation without evidence.
  15. Giving recommendations with no owner, implementation or KPI.
