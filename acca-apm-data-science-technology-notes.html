1. What has changed and why it mattersLink to this section

The official September 2026–June 2027 syllabus creates a distinct section:

D. Data science and technology for performance and insights 1. Technology and information systems 2. Data science and analytics

This is not optional or a minor topic. Under the new exam structure:

  • Section A: one 50-mark case study (40 technical + 10 professional skills), mainly drawing across syllabus sections A and B.
  • Section B: two compulsory 25-mark scenario questions.
  • One Section B question will come from Section C and one will come from Section D.
  • Therefore, expect one compulsory 25-mark data science/technology question: normally 20 technical marks and 5 professional-skills marks.
  • A Section B question examines at least two of: analysis and evaluation, scepticism, and commercial acumen.

Section D can still integrate foundational ideas from A and B, including strategy, CSFs/KPIs, risk, budgeting, customer performance, quality, sustainability, value creation and performance improvement.

2. The golden rule for Section D answersLink to this section

Do not write a generic essay about technology. Every point should follow:

Issue/feature → application to scenario → performance consequence → judgement/recommendation

Example:

Real-time RFID data would show the retailer where inventory is located and identify stock-outs sooner. This supports its CSF of product availability and should reduce lost sales. However, the benefit depends on accurate tagging and integration with the ERP system; otherwise inaccurate live data may cause inappropriate replenishment. A pilot in the highest-volume stores, supported by exception reports and periodic physical checks, is therefore preferable.

This earns more than: “RFID gives real-time data and improves efficiency.”

PART I — TECHNOLOGY AND INFORMATION SYSTEMSLink to this section

3. How IT developments influence performance management systems (D1a)Link to this section

Technology changes:

  • what can be measured: financial plus operational, customer, employee, process, environmental and behavioural data;
  • when it can be measured: periodic/batch reporting becomes near-real-time monitoring;
  • how much can be measured: complete populations and detailed transactions rather than small samples;
  • where information can be accessed: remotely and across divisions/supply chains;
  • who can obtain it: operational managers may self-serve rather than wait for finance;
  • how decisions are made: descriptive reporting is supplemented by prediction, optimisation and automation.

Performance-management benefitsLink to this section

  1. More timely detection of adverse trends and quicker corrective action.
  2. Greater accuracy through automated capture and reduced manual re-entry.
  3. Better integration of financial and non-financial performance.
  4. More granular analysis by product, customer, channel, location, process or employee.
  5. Improved forecasting, target setting and resource allocation.
  6. Exception reporting allows managers to focus on material deviations.
  7. Better organisational learning and knowledge sharing.
  8. More consistent KPIs across divisions and locations.
  9. Better visibility across a value chain or strategic alliance.
  10. More responsive customer service and personalised offerings.

Risks and limitationsLink to this section

  1. Information overload may obscure the few measures critical to strategy.
  2. Poor-quality data produces misleading analysis: “garbage in, garbage out”.
  3. Excessive monitoring may damage trust, motivation and organisational culture.
  4. Automated decisions may lack human context or judgement.
  5. Greater connectivity raises cyber, privacy and continuity risks.
  6. Systems may encourage short-term optimisation of measured KPIs at the expense of strategy.
  7. Significant implementation, training, migration and maintenance cost.
  8. Resistance to change, skills gaps and job-displacement concerns.
  9. Vendor dependence and obsolete technology.
  10. Benefits may be overstated unless they are linked to CSFs, decisions and value creation.

4. Unified corporate databasesLink to this section

A unified corporate database stores organisation-wide data in a common database accessible by authorised functions and ERP modules. It creates a single source of truth rather than separate departmental records.

BenefitsLink to this section

  • One version of customer, supplier, employee, inventory and financial data.
  • Less duplicate entry, reconciliation and inconsistency.
  • Faster consolidation and reporting across divisions.
  • Cross-functional analysis, such as linking complaints, delivery performance and customer profitability.
  • Better process management: sales information can trigger production, purchasing and accounting entries.
  • Consistent definitions of KPIs and master data.
  • Supports real-time dashboards and drill-down.

ProblemsLink to this section

  • A single error can spread throughout the organisation.
  • A single database may become a critical point of failure or attractive cyber target.
  • Integrating legacy systems and standardising data definitions is difficult.
  • Divisions may resist giving up local systems or autonomy.
  • Access must be restricted; not every user should see all employee/customer data.
  • Implementation can be expensive and disruptive.

Exam judgementLink to this section

Recommend governance: common data definitions, validation at source, role-based access, segregation of duties, backups, a data owner for each field and staged migration. A unified database is valuable only if reliable and relevant information reaches the right decision-maker.

5. Data warehousesLink to this section

A data warehouse is a central repository that collects data from multiple operational systems and external sources for reporting and analysis. Data is normally extracted, transformed and loaded (ETL) before analysis.

Difference from a unified databaseLink to this section

  • A unified database is normally the common live operational database.
  • A data warehouse combines historical data from multiple current/legacy/external sources, optimised for analytics rather than daily transaction processing.

BenefitsLink to this section

  • Consolidates fragmented data without immediately replacing every source system.
  • Holds historical information for trends, benchmarking and forecasting.
  • Cleaning and transformation improve consistency.
  • Allows complex analysis without slowing operational systems.
  • Supports dashboards, data mining and machine learning.

RisksLink to this section

  • Data may be stale if uploads are periodic.
  • Transformation rules can introduce error or bias.
  • Duplicate, missing or incompatible data may remain.
  • Storage of large amounts of personal data creates security, retention and ethical risks.
  • Cost may exceed value if no clear decision need exists.

6. Process automation and robotic process automation (RPA)Link to this section

Process automation uses technology to perform activities previously completed manually. RPA uses software “bots” to perform repeatable, rules-based computer tasks, such as transferring invoice data or reconciling accounts.

Suitable activitiesLink to this section

  • high volume;
  • repetitive and rules-based;
  • stable and standardised;
  • based on structured digital inputs;
  • low need for judgement;
  • measurable error/processing cost.

BenefitsLink to this section

  • faster throughput and 24/7 processing;
  • lower labour and error costs;
  • consistent performance and stronger audit trail;
  • fewer manual hand-offs;
  • employees released for analysis and business partnering;
  • real-time processing improves reporting timeliness;
  • scalability during volume peaks.

RisksLink to this section

  • Automation can perform a bad process faster: redesign before automating.
  • Bots fail when interfaces, formats or rules change.
  • Incorrect rules create systematic errors across many transactions.
  • Reduced human review can allow unusual transactions to pass.
  • Job losses and fear may cause resistance or reduced morale.
  • Setup, maintenance and control cost.
  • Automation bias: staff may trust output without challenge.

Controls/recommendationsLink to this section

Process mapping; eliminate non-value activities first; pilot; exception reports; human approval for high-risk items; change controls; access restrictions for bot credentials; reconciliations; performance measures such as cost per transaction, cycle time, exception rate and error rate.

7. Artificial intelligence (AI)Link to this section

AI is the capability of machines to perform tasks associated with human intelligence, such as recognising patterns, interpreting language, reasoning or recommending decisions.

  • Narrow/weak AI: designed for a specific task; this is what organisations currently use.
  • General/strong AI: human-like intelligence across many tasks; it is not the practical focus of APM.

Performance-management applicationsLink to this section

  • demand, cash-flow and cost forecasting;
  • customer churn or default prediction;
  • fraud/anomaly detection;
  • dynamic pricing and product recommendation;
  • predictive maintenance;
  • automated classification of invoices or customer messages;
  • scenario modelling and resource optimisation;
  • generation of management-report summaries, subject to review.

EvaluationLink to this section

AI may process more variables and detect non-linear patterns humans miss, improving speed and predictive accuracy. But its output depends on relevant, representative, lawful data and a model suited to the objective. Black-box models, bias, concept drift and over-reliance can make decisions worse. Human accountability remains necessary.

8. RFID, sensors and the Internet of ThingsLink to this section

A radio-frequency identification device (RFID) stores identification data in a tag which can be read wirelessly. Unlike a barcode, it may be read without direct line of sight and multiple tags may be scanned quickly.

The Internet of Things (IoT) consists of physical devices/sensors connected to networks that collect and transmit data.

ApplicationsLink to this section

  • live tracking of inventory, assets and deliveries;
  • automatic stock counts and replenishment;
  • monitoring temperature in food/pharmaceutical storage;
  • monitoring machine vibration, energy use and downtime;
  • tracing items across the supply chain;
  • tracking patient/equipment location in healthcare;
  • measuring footfall and customer movement.

Performance benefitsLink to this section

  • improved inventory accuracy and availability;
  • reduced theft, waste and stockholding;
  • shorter cycle times;
  • preventative/predictive maintenance and less downtime;
  • better quality and regulatory traceability;
  • data for supplier SLAs and sustainability measures.

RisksLink to this section

  • tag/sensor and network costs;
  • inaccurate, damaged or duplicated readings;
  • large volumes of low-value data;
  • integration problems;
  • cyberattack through insecure devices;
  • employee/customer surveillance and privacy concerns;
  • reliance on connectivity.

9. Cloud and network technologyLink to this section

Cloud computing provides remote computing resources over a network. Common forms are:

  • SaaS: use provider software, e.g. cloud accounting/CRM;
  • PaaS: use a platform to build/deploy applications;
  • IaaS: rent computing, storage and network infrastructure.

BenefitsLink to this section

  • lower upfront capital expenditure and usage-based costs;
  • rapid deployment and scalability;
  • remote access and collaboration;
  • easier standardisation across locations;
  • provider-managed upgrades, backup and resilience;
  • access to sophisticated tools otherwise unaffordable to smaller organisations.

RisksLink to this section

  • internet outages and service-provider failure;
  • cybersecurity, confidentiality and data-location concerns;
  • vendor lock-in and price increases;
  • less direct control over infrastructure;
  • contractual issues over service levels, ownership, deletion and recovery;
  • migration/integration difficulties;
  • variable cost can grow if usage is uncontrolled.

Appropriate controlsLink to this section

Due diligence on provider; service-level agreement; encryption; multifactor authentication; backup/recovery testing; exit plan and data portability; monitoring usage/cost; legal review of data jurisdiction; clear shared-responsibility model.

10. Data silos (D1b)Link to this section

A data silo is a collection of data controlled by one department/system and not readily accessible, integrated or understood elsewhere.

CausesLink to this section

  • separate legacy systems;
  • departmental culture and protection of information;
  • inconsistent data formats/definitions;
  • acquisitions and geographically dispersed operations;
  • poor governance and unclear ownership;
  • incompatible software or lack of integration investment.

Problems for the accounting functionLink to this section

  1. Duplicate data entry and avoidable labour cost.
  2. Time-consuming reconciliations and month-end delays.
  3. Conflicting figures undermine confidence in reports.
  4. Incomplete view of customer, product or process profitability.
  5. Difficulties linking financial outcomes to operational drivers.
  6. Inconsistent KPIs and targets between divisions.
  7. Poor forecasting and resource allocation.
  8. Errors due to manual spreadsheets and transfers.
  9. Finance remains a scorekeeper rather than strategic partner.
  10. Fraud or poor performance may remain hidden between systems.
  11. Harder regulatory, sustainability and integrated reporting.
  12. Local optimisation: one department improves its own KPI while harming total performance.

SolutionsLink to this section

  • ERP/unified database or data warehouse;
  • application programming interfaces (APIs) between systems;
  • data dictionary and common KPI definitions;
  • master-data management;
  • data owners/stewards and governance committee;
  • role-based access rather than blanket secrecy;
  • reconciliation and data-quality dashboards;
  • cultural incentives for collaboration.

Do not recommend removing all restrictions: legitimate confidentiality and segregation-of-duties controls remain essential.

11. Enterprise resource planning systems (ERP) (D1c)Link to this section

An ERP system integrates core processes and information through linked modules, normally supported by common data.

Typical modules: finance/general ledger, purchasing, inventory, production/MRP, sales, payroll/HR and CRM.

Performance-management benefitsLink to this section

  • integrated end-to-end view of processes;
  • real-time information and faster closing/reporting;
  • fewer duplicate records and reconciliations;
  • standard processes and KPIs across locations;
  • automated controls and audit trails;
  • drill-down from summary KPI to transaction;
  • better planning of materials, capacity and cash;
  • supports ABC/ABM and process-based management;
  • finance can focus on interpretation rather than data production.

Limitations/implementation risksLink to this section

  • high cost, long implementation and uncertain benefits;
  • disruption and project failure risk;
  • forcing a standard process may not suit unique operations;
  • data migration and master-data errors;
  • training needs and employee resistance;
  • excessive customisation increases cost and upgrade difficulty;
  • dependence on supplier/consultant;
  • integrated error/cyberattack has organisation-wide effect.

Recommendation frameworkLink to this section

Business case linked to strategy; process redesign; stakeholder consultation; data cleansing; phased rollout; training; clear ownership; parallel run where necessary; post-implementation review using measures such as report lead time, error rate, system availability, user adoption and realised savings.

12. Knowledge management systems (KMS) (D1c)Link to this section

A KMS captures, organises and shares organisational knowledge.

  • Explicit knowledge: can be documented—manuals, procedures, rules and designs.
  • Tacit knowledge: experience and judgement held by people, harder to codify.
  • Codification approach: information stored in databases, wikis and document libraries.
  • Personalisation approach: connects people through expert directories, forums and communities.

BenefitsLink to this section

  • avoids knowledge loss when staff leave;
  • speeds employee training and problem resolution;
  • shares good practice between locations;
  • reduces duplicated work;
  • supports innovation, quality and customer service;
  • improves consistency and organisational learning.

Limitations/controlsLink to this section

Information can become obsolete, inaccurate or excessive. Staff may not contribute because of time, lack of reward or fear of losing power. Tacit knowledge may lose context when documented. Use named owners, review dates, version controls, search/tagging, access controls, quality review and incentives for useful contribution. Measure usage, resolution time, repeated-error rate and user satisfaction—not simply documents uploaded.

13. Customer relationship management systems (CRM) (D1c)Link to this section

A CRM system manages customer data and interactions through acquisition, conversion, service, retention and extension/cross-selling.

Data and possible KPIsLink to this section

  • leads, conversion rate and customer-acquisition cost;
  • purchase history and average order value;
  • contact/complaint records and resolution time;
  • churn/retention and repeat-purchase rate;
  • customer lifetime value and customer profitability;
  • campaign response and return on marketing expenditure;
  • customer satisfaction and net promoter score.

BenefitsLink to this section

  • a complete customer view across channels;
  • more targeted marketing and personalised service;
  • earlier identification of churn risk;
  • better allocation of sales effort;
  • improved retention and customer lifetime value;
  • links non-financial customer drivers to revenue/profit.

RisksLink to this section

  • inaccurate or duplicate customer records;
  • excessive contact and reputational damage;
  • sales staff may manipulate pipeline data;
  • intrusive profiling, consent and privacy concerns;
  • biased algorithms may exclude or exploit customers;
  • focusing on easy-to-measure transactions can weaken relationships.

Evaluation tipLink to this section

A CRM is not automatically successful. Assess whether it improves a relevant CSF and creates financial value after implementation, data, training and compliance costs.

14. Systems and data risks, methods and controls (D1d)Link to this section

Use this structure: risk → impact on performance/information → specific control → residual limitation.

Main risksLink to this section

  • unauthorised access and privilege abuse;
  • phishing/social engineering and stolen credentials;
  • malware/ransomware;
  • denial of service and network failure;
  • accidental deletion or incorrect entry;
  • insider theft/fraud;
  • device loss;
  • software defects and unauthorised changes;
  • third-party/cloud/supply-chain failure;
  • physical fire, flood or power loss;
  • inadequate backup or disaster recovery;
  • privacy breach and unlawful processing;
  • inaccurate, incomplete, duplicated, untimely or biased data;
  • loss of availability, confidentiality or integrity.

Preventive controlsLink to this section

  • least-privilege, role-based access;
  • strong passwords and multifactor authentication;
  • segregation of duties;
  • encryption in transit and at rest;
  • firewalls, anti-malware and secure configuration;
  • patch management and penetration testing;
  • employee awareness and phishing training;
  • input validation, mandatory fields and reasonableness checks;
  • supplier due diligence and contractual security clauses;
  • physical access controls;
  • privacy-by-design and data minimisation;
  • authorised change-management procedures.

Detective controlsLink to this section

  • audit logs and review of privileged activity;
  • intrusion detection and security monitoring;
  • exception/anomaly reports;
  • reconciliations and control totals;
  • data-quality dashboards;
  • vulnerability scans;
  • periodic access review;
  • incident reporting and internal audit.

Corrective/recovery controlsLink to this section

  • incident-response plan and assigned responsibilities;
  • isolated, versioned and tested backups;
  • disaster-recovery site and business-continuity plan;
  • restore testing, not merely making backups;
  • failover/redundancy;
  • cyber insurance where cost effective;
  • lessons-learned review and remediation;
  • lawful notification of regulators/affected persons.

Core security objectivesLink to this section

  • Confidentiality: information only seen by authorised users.
  • Integrity: information is complete, accurate and not improperly altered.
  • Availability: systems/data accessible when needed.

Controls involve cost and cannot eliminate risk. Recommendations should reflect risk appetite, probability, impact, legal duties and the value/sensitivity of information.

15. Lean management information systems and the 5 Ss (D1e)Link to this section

A lean information system provides only information that adds value to users, at the right time and quality, while eliminating waste. Lean does not mean “cheap” or simply fewer reports.

Features:

  • reports only where they support decisions;
  • reports sent only to users who need them;
  • timely/real-time processing where its benefit exceeds cost;
  • data entered once and reused;
  • flexible drill-down and ad-hoc reporting;
  • regular review and continuous improvement.

The 5 Ss applied to informationLink to this section

Naming differs slightly across texts, so understand the action rather than memorising one translation:

  1. Sort (structurise): identify information essential for planning, control and decisions; remove obsolete, duplicated and unused reports/data. Example: discontinue a weekly report no manager reads.
  2. Simplify (systemise/set in order): organise data and communication so users can find and understand it. Use clear dashboards, logical folders, searchable metadata and a single source of truth.
  3. Scan (sanitise/shine): regularly clean and audit data, reports and usage. Correct errors; remove obsolete customer/product records; check which reports are actually used.
  4. Standardise (stabilise): consistent KPI definitions, report formats, frequency, responsibilities and data-entry rules across the organisation.
  5. Sustain (self-discipline): make the first four continuous through ownership, training, review schedules, feedback and performance monitoring.

Information valueLink to this section

Information is valuable if it is relevant, reliable, timely, understandable, complete enough, comparable, accessible to authorised users and worth more than its cost.

Questions to ask:

  • Which decision will this information change?
  • Which strategic objective/CSF does it monitor?
  • Who needs it, how often and at what detail?
  • Is real-time data worth its extra cost?
  • Can users drill down from exceptions?
  • Is 90% accurate now more useful than 100% accurate too late?
  • Does the report create action, or only administrative work?

Implementation problemsLink to this section

  • managers retain familiar reports “just in case”;
  • users disagree about what is valuable;
  • removal may eliminate data later needed for trends/compliance;
  • inconsistent definitions across departments;
  • system inflexibility or poor integration;
  • poor-quality legacy data;
  • lack of senior support and continuous discipline;
  • fear that transparency exposes poor performance;
  • initial cleansing/training cost.

Examiner lessonLink to this section

If asked about the 5 Ss, explain each one in the scenario, give an example and assess implementation issues. Do not merely list five definitions.

PART II — DATA SCIENCE AND ANALYTICSLink to this section

16. Core definitionsLink to this section

  • Data: raw facts, observations or signals.
  • Information: data processed and placed in context so it becomes useful.
  • Insight: an interpretation of information that explains an issue or supports action.
  • Data analytics: collecting and examining data to extract meaningful insights for decisions and performance improvement.
  • Data science: a wider, iterative discipline combining business understanding, statistics, computing and domain knowledge to solve problems using data.
  • Algorithm: a defined set of rules/instructions used to solve a problem or complete a task.
  • Model: a simplified quantitative representation that uses input variables to estimate, classify, explain or optimise an outcome.
  • Data mining: examining large datasets to discover patterns, associations, sequences, clusters or anomalies.

In APM, the purpose is not technical programming. The management accountant should:

  1. define the performance problem and required decision;
  2. ensure the data and model are fit for that purpose;
  3. interpret the output commercially;
  4. challenge limitations and uncertainty;
  5. communicate insight clearly;
  6. recommend proportionate action;
  7. ensure ethical use and accountability.

17. Big data and its impact (D2a)Link to this section

Big data refers to datasets whose size, speed and complexity require enhanced methods of storage, processing and analysis. The familiar characteristics are the Vs:

  1. Volume: very large quantities of transactions, sensor readings, clicks, messages and other observations.
  2. Velocity: data arrives and may need processing rapidly or in real time.
  3. Variety: structured and unstructured data from many internal/external sources.
  4. Veracity: reliability, accuracy and credibility of the data.
  5. Value: whether analysis generates benefits or better decisions exceeding its cost.

Other useful concerns include variability (changing meaning/patterns), vulnerability (security/privacy risk) and visualisation, but do not force extra Vs into every answer.

Structured, semi-structured and unstructured dataLink to this section

  • Structured: organised into defined fields and tables, such as sales date, product code, units and price. Easy to query and aggregate.
  • Semi-structured: has tags or some organisation but no fixed relational table, such as JSON, XML or email metadata.
  • Unstructured: free-form text, image, audio, video, documents and social-media content. Rich but harder to process and verify.

Internal dataLink to this section

Sales, costs, ERP transactions, budgets, production, quality, employee data, web activity, CRM interactions and sensor readings. It may be directly relevant and controllable but can reflect existing organisational biases and omit competitors or external change.

External dataLink to this section

Economic indicators, weather, competitor prices, demographics, market research, social media, satellite/geospatial data and public datasets. It broadens analysis but may have uncertain definitions, provenance, legality, timeliness or reliability.

How big data improves performance managementLink to this section

  • more detailed customer segmentation and personalisation;
  • demand forecasting and dynamic pricing;
  • early detection of churn, fraud, defects and machine failure;
  • optimisation of inventory, routes, capacity and staffing;
  • real-time KPIs and exception management;
  • measurement of non-financial drivers before financial effects appear;
  • whole-population analysis rather than reliance on samples;
  • identification of new products, markets and revenue streams;
  • improved customer experience and competitive advantage;
  • sustainability measurement through energy, waste and supply-chain data.

Risks and challengesLink to this section

  1. Cost/value: infrastructure, specialist staff, cleaning and compliance may exceed benefits.
  2. Veracity: inaccurate, incomplete, duplicated, stale or manipulated data leads to bad decisions.
  3. Relevance: more data is not necessarily more useful; spurious patterns become more likely.
  4. Integration: sources use incompatible definitions, formats and time periods.
  5. Skills: shortage of data scientists and managers capable of interpretation.
  6. Security/privacy: larger datasets are attractive targets and may enable intrusive profiling.
  7. Regulation: collection, use, transfer and retention may be restricted.
  8. Bias: historic or unrepresentative data embeds unfairness.
  9. Correlation: a relationship does not establish cause.
  10. Model risk: overfitting, weak assumptions and changing conditions reduce reliability.
  11. Information overload: managers lose focus on strategic CSFs.
  12. Behavioural impact: surveillance can reduce trust or cause dysfunctional behaviour.
  13. Reputation: lawful data use can still be perceived as unfair or intrusive.
  14. Environmental cost: large-scale processing consumes energy and resources.

A balanced exam conclusionLink to this section

Big data has no intrinsic value. It creates value only where reliable and ethically obtained data is converted into relevant insight, action and measurable progress toward strategy. Begin with the decision—not with all available data.

18. The data-science process (D2b)Link to this section

A strong answer uses an iterative process. A useful memory aid is:

Goal → Get → Govern → Clean → Transform → Store → Analyse → Validate → Communicate → Act/monitor

Step 1: Define the business goal and decisionLink to this section

  • Start with mission, strategic objectives, CSFs and stakeholder needs.
  • State a precise question, e.g. “Which subscribers are at high risk of cancelling next month, so retention offers can be targeted profitably?”
  • Define the target/output and success criteria.
  • Identify constraints, affected stakeholders, ethical issues and cost-benefit threshold.

Weak goal: “Use AI to analyse customers.”

Strong goal: “Reduce controllable customer churn from 8% to 6% without reducing contribution after retention discounts.”

Step 2: Select and obtain dataLink to this section

  • Determine relevant financial and non-financial variables.
  • Select internal/external and structured/unstructured sources.
  • Check legal authority, consent, ownership, purpose and provenance.
  • Avoid collecting fields merely because they are available.
  • Use an appropriate time horizon and sufficiently representative observations.

Possible churn inputs: tenure, usage, complaints, payment failures, service outages, contract type and prior offers. Protected or proxy characteristics may be ethically inappropriate.

Step 3: Understand and assess data qualityLink to this section

Profile:

  • completeness;
  • accuracy;
  • validity against rules;
  • consistency across sources;
  • uniqueness/duplicates;
  • timeliness;
  • representativeness;
  • granularity and units;
  • possible selection/measurement bias.

Step 4: Clean the dataLink to this section

  • remove or merge duplicates;
  • correct obvious errors and inconsistent coding;
  • standardise dates, currencies, names and units;
  • investigate outliers rather than automatically deleting them;
  • deal transparently with missing values—exclude, obtain, impute or flag;
  • reconcile key totals with controlled sources;
  • document all changes for reproducibility and audit.

Cleaning can create bias. For example, deleting all incomplete customer records may disproportionately remove a particular channel or demographic group.

Step 5: Transform and prepareLink to this section

  • aggregate/disaggregate to suitable detail;
  • join sources using valid identifiers;
  • encode categories;
  • scale variables where necessary;
  • derive meaningful features, e.g. complaint frequency or days since last purchase;
  • anonymise or pseudonymise personal identifiers;
  • split data for model training, validation and testing;
  • retain lineage from source to output.

Step 6: Store and protectLink to this section

Choose operational database, warehouse, data lake or cloud according to volume, variety, speed and use. Apply access controls, encryption, backup, retention/deletion rules, version control and data ownership.

A data lake stores large quantities of data in relatively raw form for flexible future analysis. It can become a poorly governed “data swamp” if metadata, quality and ownership are weak.

Step 7: Analyse/build the modelLink to this section

Select descriptive, diagnostic, predictive or prescriptive analysis according to the goal. Keep a simple model where it meets the need; complexity should earn its extra cost and loss of explainability.

Step 8: Validate and challengeLink to this section

  • test on data not used to train the model;
  • compare with a baseline/current process;
  • assess error rates and business cost of each error;
  • check assumptions, sensitivity, bias and plausibility;
  • obtain domain-expert and user review;
  • document limitations.

Step 9: Communicate insightsLink to this section

Explain in non-technical language:

  • what the model says;
  • strength and uncertainty of evidence;
  • why it matters to objectives/KPIs;
  • actions available and costs/risks;
  • limitations and further information needed.

Step 10: Implement, monitor and refineLink to this section

Use a pilot or controlled experiment where possible. Track realised benefits, unexpected outcomes, data drift and model drift. Establish ownership and review triggers. Models are not “set and forget”.

19. Four types of analytics (D2c)Link to this section

19.1 Descriptive analytics — What happened/is happening?Link to this section

Uses current and historic data to summarise performance.

Methods: totals, averages, ratios, trends, dashboards, distributions and visualisations.

Examples:

  • monthly revenue fell 8%;
  • churn is 6%;
  • defect rate is highest on line 3;
  • customer complaints increased in the north region.

Benefits: accessible; timely overview; identifies trends/outliers; supports reporting and benchmarking.

Limitations: does not explain cause; aggregation can hide variation; backward-looking; choice of average/graph may mislead.

19.2 Diagnostic analytics — Why did it happen?Link to this section

Investigates drivers/root causes.

Methods: drill-down, variance analysis, segmentation, data mining, correlation, regression, time-series analysis, process analysis and “five whys”.

Example: revenue fell because mobile-site failures raised checkout abandonment, particularly among new customers.

Benefits: focuses corrective action on drivers rather than symptoms; combines financial and non-financial information.

Limitations: correlation is not causation; omitted variables; historic relationships may not persist; management may select an explanation supporting its interests.

19.3 Predictive analytics — What is likely to happen?Link to this section

Uses historical/current data, statistics and machine learning to estimate future outcomes and probabilities.

Examples: demand forecast; probability a customer will churn; likelihood of equipment failure; expected credit loss.

Benefits: proactive planning; better targets, budgets, inventory and risk management; permits scenarios and early intervention.

Limitations: predictions are uncertain; rare shocks/regime change; biased training data; overfitting; false positives/negatives; past patterns may not continue.

19.4 Prescriptive analytics — What should we do?Link to this section

Recommends actions, often using optimisation, simulation, business rules and predicted outcomes subject to constraints.

Examples: optimal price, route, staffing, inventory reorder or retention offer.

Benefits: connects insight to action; evaluates trade-offs; may improve resource allocation and consistency.

Limitations: recommendation reflects the objective and constraints programmed. Maximising profit may harm service, ethics or resilience. It can create false certainty and reduce human accountability.

How the four work togetherLink to this section

Retail example:

  • Descriptive: stock-outs rose to 9%.
  • Diagnostic: supplier delay and inaccurate store inventory caused the rise.
  • Predictive: model forecasts which products/stores will stock out next week.
  • Prescriptive: system recommends transfer/reorder quantities given lead times, storage and margin.

Always identify which analysis answers the scenario’s question. Do not recommend predictive analytics when management only needs a reliable description of current performance.

20. Types of data used in analytics (D2d)Link to this section

20.1 Text analyticsLink to this section

Converts text—emails, reviews, complaints, social posts, surveys and support tickets—into categories, themes or quantitative measures.

Applications:

  • topic detection and recurring complaint causes;
  • sentiment (positive, neutral, negative);
  • urgency or complaint routing;
  • monitoring brand reputation;
  • identifying product defects or employee concerns.

Benefits: analyses large volumes faster and consistently; captures qualitative customer/employee information; reveals issues before financial effects.

Risks: sarcasm, dialect, multilingual content and context can be misclassified; vocal minorities may not represent customers; bots/fake reviews; consent and privacy; sentiment is not the same as purchase behaviour.

20.2 Image analyticsLink to this section

Algorithms recognise objects, features or conditions in images.

Applications: visual quality inspection, damaged-product detection, shelf availability, medical images, crop conditions and document/invoice capture.

Benefits: speed, consistent inspection, large-scale monitoring and earlier defect detection.

Risks: lighting/angle/poor images; false classification; privacy/biometric concerns; biased training images; human review needed for high-impact cases.

20.3 Video analyticsLink to this section

Analyses sequences of images, objects and movement.

Applications: customer flow/queue length; store conversion; process bottlenecks; safety compliance; asset/patient movement; sports/service performance.

Benefits: adds process context that point-of-sale data cannot show; supports layout, staffing and safety improvements.

Risks: intrusive surveillance; employee stress; storage/cyber cost; mistaken identification; legal notice/consent; measurements may change behaviour.

20.4 Speech and voice analyticsLink to this section

  • Speech analytics: what was said—transcription, keywords and topics.
  • Voice analytics: how it was said—tone, volume, pitch, pace and emotion.

Applications: complaint themes; agent compliance; real-time prompts; identifying frustrated customers; training and quality control.

Risks: accents, noise, sarcasm and disability may reduce accuracy; emotion inference can be unreliable; recording consent; sensitive conversations; unfair employee scoring.

Key exam evaluationLink to this section

For any data type discuss: relevance to the CSF; representativeness; accuracy; collection cost; timeliness; integration with financial data; consent/privacy; bias; action from the insight; human verification.

21. Analysing data: patterns, trends, ranges and distributions (D2e)Link to this section

Measures of central tendencyLink to this section

  • Mean: total divided by number of observations. Uses all data but distorted by extreme values.
  • Median: middle observation. Robust to outliers; useful for skewed data such as salaries or response times.
  • Mode: most frequent category/value. Useful for popular product or common complaint.

Choosing an average affects the story. “Average waiting time” may appear acceptable while a long tail of customers suffers unacceptable delays.

Measures of spreadLink to this section

  • Range: maximum minus minimum. Simple but depends only on two observations.
  • Interquartile range: spread of middle 50%; robust to extremes.
  • Variance/standard deviation: dispersion around the mean. Higher spread means less consistency/riskier prediction.

Distribution and shapeLink to this section

  • Normal/symmetric: mean and median broadly similar.
  • Right-skewed: long high-value tail; mean exceeds median.
  • Left-skewed: long low-value tail; mean below median.
  • Bimodal/multimodal: may indicate distinct customer/process groups that should be analysed separately.

Trends, seasonality and patternsLink to this section

  • Trend: long-term direction.
  • Seasonality: recurring calendar pattern.
  • Cycle: wider economic/business movement.
  • Random variation: unpredictable noise.
  • Outlier: unusual observation—could be error, fraud, rare event or useful warning.

Do not automatically remove outliers. Investigate their source and performance significance.

Biases to identifyLink to this section

  • Selection bias: sample systematically excludes some population.
  • Survivorship bias: only successful/remaining cases considered.
  • Measurement bias: collection method systematically misstates data.
  • Response/self-selection bias: respondents differ from non-respondents.
  • Confirmation bias: analyst favours evidence supporting an existing view.
  • Historical bias: data reflects past inequality or policies.
  • Sampling bias: sample not representative.
  • Automation bias: unjustified trust in system output.
  • Proxy bias: apparently neutral variable indirectly represents a sensitive trait.
  • Aggregation bias/Simpson’s paradox: overall relationship differs within subgroups.
  • Look-ahead/data leakage: model uses information that would not be available when prediction is made.

22. Regression analysis (D2e)Link to this section

Regression estimates the relationship between a dependent outcome and one or more independent/explanatory variables.

Simple linear regressionLink to this section

y = a + bx

  • y: predicted dependent variable, e.g. sales.
  • x: explanatory variable, e.g. advertising spend.
  • a/intercept: predicted y when x is zero.
  • b/slope/coefficient: estimated change in y for a one-unit increase in x.

Example: Sales (£000) = 200 + 4.5 × advertising (£000)

Interpretation: within the observed data/model, an additional £1,000 advertising is associated with £4,500 higher sales. It does not prove advertising caused the increase.

Multiple regressionLink to this section

y = a + b₁x₁ + b₂x₂ + … + bₙxₙ

It estimates the association of each variable with y while holding included variables constant. For demand, variables might include price, advertising, income, season and competitor price.

Key output to interpretLink to this section

  • Coefficient: direction and size of estimated relationship. Check units and commercial significance.
  • R / correlation coefficient: direction and strength of linear association, from −1 to +1 in simple contexts.
  • R² (coefficient of determination): proportion of variation in y explained by the model. A high R² is not proof of causation, accuracy out of sample or a good decision model.
  • Adjusted R²: penalises unnecessary variables and can help compare multiple models.
  • p-value/significance: evidence against a zero relationship under statistical assumptions. Statistical significance does not equal material business value.
  • Confidence interval: plausible range for a coefficient/estimate; wide intervals signal uncertainty.
  • Residual: actual minus predicted value. Patterns in residuals may reveal model problems.
  • Prediction interval: range for a future observation, normally wider than confidence around an average.

Questions to ask before relying on regressionLink to this section

  1. Is the relationship plausible and relevant to the business?
  2. Is it linear, or is another form needed?
  3. Does the sample cover the population/time period of the decision?
  4. Are important variables omitted?
  5. Are explanatory variables highly related to each other (multicollinearity)?
  6. Are errors independent and reasonably stable in spread?
  7. Are outliers driving the equation?
  8. Has the model been validated out of sample?
  9. Is management extrapolating beyond observed x values?
  10. Have price inflation, seasonality or structural change been addressed?
  11. Does improvement versus a simple baseline justify complexity?
  12. What are the costs of forecast errors?

Common regression trapsLink to this section

  • Correlation is not causation: ice-cream sales and swimming incidents may both rise due to hot weather.
  • Spurious correlation: with many variables, some relationships occur by chance.
  • Omitted-variable bias: a missing factor affects both x and y.
  • Reverse causality: high sales may cause advertising spend, not only the reverse.
  • Overfitting: model fits historic noise and performs poorly on new data.
  • Extrapolation: relationship outside the observed range may not hold.
  • Structural break/concept drift: market, customer or process has changed.

Exam-style interpretationLink to this section

Do not merely say “R² is high, so the model is good.” Say:

The model explains 82% of the historic variation in weekly demand, suggesting it could improve inventory planning. However, the remaining 18% and the prediction interval must be considered because stock-outs are costly. The data covers only a stable pre-entry period and may not represent demand after the new competitor launches. Management should validate the model using recent hold-out data, compare forecast errors with the existing method and use scenario ranges rather than a single point forecast.

23. Machine learning and AI for insight and improvement (D2f)Link to this section

Machine learning (ML) is a branch of AI in which algorithms learn patterns from data rather than relying only on explicitly programmed decision rules.

Main formsLink to this section

  • Supervised learning: trained using labelled outcomes. Uses include sales prediction, churn, fraud and classification.
  • Unsupervised learning: finds patterns without labelled outcomes. Uses include customer clustering, unusual transaction detection and association analysis.
  • Reinforcement learning: learns actions through rewards/penalties in an environment. Potential uses include dynamic routing or pricing, but control is important.

Common tasksLink to this section

  • Regression: predicts a continuous amount, e.g. demand or cost.
  • Classification: predicts a category/probability, e.g. churn/no churn.
  • Clustering: groups similar observations, e.g. customer segments.
  • Anomaly detection: identifies unusual transactions or process readings.
  • Recommendation: predicts items/content a user may prefer.
  • Natural language processing: analyses or generates language.
  • Computer vision: analyses images/video.

BenefitsLink to this section

  • handles large datasets and many variables;
  • identifies complex/non-linear patterns;
  • improves through retraining with new information;
  • enables faster and more personalised decisions;
  • supports earlier intervention and efficient resource use;
  • automates repetitive analysis;
  • can improve forecasting and fraud/defect detection.

Limitations and risksLink to this section

  • high data, skill and computing requirements;
  • black-box lack of explainability;
  • biased training data and unfair outcomes;
  • overfitting and data leakage;
  • false positives and negatives;
  • model/concept drift;
  • cybersecurity and adversarial manipulation;
  • unclear accountability;
  • users may over-trust recommendations;
  • objective may be too narrow, e.g. maximising clicks rather than value/wellbeing;
  • generative AI may invent plausible but false information (“hallucination”), expose confidential input or reproduce intellectual property.

Human-in-the-loopLink to this section

Use human review where decisions are material, unusual, legally sensitive or affect individuals. Human review must be meaningful—not automatic approval. Reviewers need understandable reasons, authority to override and monitoring of override patterns.

Performance measures for classification modelsLink to this section

Use a confusion matrix:

  • True positive (TP): correctly predicts the event.
  • False positive (FP): predicts event when it does not occur.
  • True negative (TN): correctly predicts no event.
  • False negative (FN): fails to predict an event.

Metrics:

  • Accuracy = (TP + TN) / all cases. Misleading for rare events.
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP). Of predicted positives, how many were correct?
  • Recall/sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN). Of actual positives, how many were found?
  • Specificity = TN / (TN + FP). Of actual negatives, how many were correctly rejected?

Commercial interpretation matters more than metric memorisation. In fraud detection, a false negative may lose money; too many false positives block genuine customers and damage service. Choose a probability threshold reflecting these asymmetric costs and risk appetite.

Performance measures for forecastsLink to this section

  • mean absolute error (MAE): average absolute error, easy to interpret;
  • mean squared/root mean squared error (MSE/RMSE): penalises large errors more;
  • mean absolute percentage error (MAPE): percentage interpretation but problematic near zero;
  • forecast bias: whether forecasts systematically over/understate;
  • operational consequences: stock-outs, waste, idle capacity and contribution lost.

24. Assessing and refining data-model output (D2g)Link to this section

Use the mnemonic GOALS:

  • G — Goal alignment: Does output answer the original decision and strategic objective?
  • O — Output performance: Accuracy, error, stability and improvement over baseline.
  • A — Assumptions and accountability: Are assumptions reasonable, explainable and owned?
  • L — Legitimacy: Relevant, representative, lawful and ethical data; bias/fairness checked.
  • S — Sustainability: Cost, skills, integration, security, monitoring and ability to update.

Full evaluation checklistLink to this section

  1. Purpose: Is it prediction, explanation, classification or optimisation? A predictive model need not prove causality.
  2. Relevance: Does output match the correct customer/process/time horizon and KPI?
  3. Data quality: completeness, accuracy, consistency, timeliness and provenance.
  4. Representativeness: all affected groups, seasons, regions and conditions included?
  5. Validation: tested on independent/hold-out data?
  6. Baseline: better than existing method or a simple rule?
  7. Error trade-off: operational and ethical costs of false outcomes?
  8. Uncertainty: intervals/scenarios shown rather than false precision?
  9. Robustness: stable if assumptions or inputs change?
  10. Explainability: can management and affected users understand the key drivers?
  11. Fairness: systematically different error/outcome rates across groups?
  12. Causality: is management incorrectly treating association as cause?
  13. Actionability: is there a feasible action that creates value?
  14. Integration: fits workflows, controls and existing systems?
  15. Economics: expected benefit exceeds full lifecycle cost?
  16. Monitoring: owner, frequency, thresholds and retraining defined?

Possible refinementsLink to this section

  • obtain more recent or representative data;
  • correct data errors or redefine variables;
  • add a relevant omitted variable;
  • remove leakage, irrelevant/proxy or unstable variables;
  • segment populations rather than use one model for all;
  • simplify the model for explainability;
  • tune thresholds to commercial error costs;
  • rebalance training data or apply fairness constraints;
  • include external/qualitative evidence;
  • use scenario/sensitivity analysis;
  • compare alternative models and baseline;
  • conduct pilot/A-B test;
  • add human review and override controls;
  • retrain when drift thresholds are breached.

25. Advising management from model output (D2h)Link to this section

A high-scoring response does not repeat model numbers. It turns output into decision support.

Recommended structure: INSIGHTLink to this section

  1. I — Issue: state the decision/problem.
  2. N — Numerical finding: quote only the relevant output/trend.
  3. S — Strategic significance: connect to objective, CSF, KPI and value.
  4. I — Interpretation and cause: explain what it may mean without overstating causation.
  5. G — Gaps/risks: uncertainty, assumptions, data limitations and stakeholder effects.
  6. H — Helpful action: specific, feasible recommendation with owner/pilot/control.
  7. T — Track: KPI, target/time and review trigger.

Example:

The churn model identifies customers with repeated outages as three times more likely to cancel. Since retention is a CSF and acquiring replacements is expensive, the company should prioritise network repair in the two affected regions and offer service recovery to high-risk, high-lifetime-value customers. The association does not prove every cancellation is caused by outages, and the model underrepresents prepaid customers. A four-week pilot should therefore compare churn and contribution against a control group, with separate error rates monitored by contract type before wider rollout.

Communicating uncertaintyLink to this section

Use “suggests”, “is associated with”, “estimated”, “subject to” and ranges where appropriate. Avoid “proves”, “will definitely” and unexplained decimal precision.

Good recommendations areLink to this section

  • linked to evidence and strategy;
  • specific about what, who and when;
  • proportionate to risk and cost;
  • sensitive to stakeholders and ethics;
  • measurable;
  • accompanied by control, pilot or review.

26. Ethical issues in information collection and processing (D2i)Link to this section

Data ethics concerns the moral obligations surrounding the collection, protection, analysis and use of data and its effects on people. Legal compliance is a minimum, not the complete ethical test.

26.1 Core ethical principlesLink to this section

  1. Purpose and intention: define a legitimate purpose before collecting data. Do not collect “just in case”.
  2. Ownership/control and consent: individuals should have appropriate control and meaningful choice over personal information. Consent should be informed and freely given where relied upon.
  3. Transparency: explain what is collected, from where, why, how long, with whom shared and how automated decisions affect people.
  4. Privacy and confidentiality: protect personal and sensitive data from inappropriate access or inference.
  5. Data minimisation: collect only data necessary for the stated purpose.
  6. Accuracy: allow correction and prevent harmful decisions based on poor data.
  7. Fairness/non-discrimination: avoid unjustified disadvantage to individuals or groups.
  8. Accountability: a named person/board remains responsible for outcomes; a machine is not accountable.
  9. Explainability and contestability: significant decisions should be understandable and challengeable, with human review/redress.
  10. Security: safeguards should match sensitivity and harm from breach.
  11. Retention limitation: keep information only as long as justified, then securely delete/anonymise.
  12. Proportionality: expected benefit should justify intrusion and potential harm.
  13. Outcome/avoidance of harm: consider unintended effects even where intentions are good.

26.2 Algorithmic biasLink to this section

Bias may arise from:

  • unrepresentative training samples;
  • historic discrimination in labels/outcomes;
  • measurement errors;
  • choice of target variable;
  • proxy variables correlated with protected characteristics;
  • feedback loops;
  • different data quality across groups;
  • design teams lacking diversity/context;
  • thresholds prioritising one stakeholder’s cost over another’s harm.

Example of feedback loop: a model directs more inspections to locations with historically more detected cases; more inspections create more detections, which causes still more future inspections, even if underlying incidence elsewhere is similar.

ControlsLink to this section

  • representative datasets and documented provenance;
  • test outcome and error rates across relevant groups;
  • remove/reconsider proxy and unnecessary sensitive variables;
  • independent ethical/bias review;
  • clear objective and fairness criteria;
  • explainability and meaningful appeal;
  • monitor outcomes after implementation;
  • avoid deploying if harm cannot be controlled.

Merely removing a protected field may not remove bias because postcode, school or purchasing history can act as proxies.

26.3 Black-box algorithmsLink to this section

A black-box model’s internal reasoning is difficult for users, auditors or affected individuals to understand.

Risks:

  • errors/bias remain hidden;
  • management cannot justify a decision;
  • difficult audit and regulatory challenge;
  • loss of trust;
  • unclear responsibility;
  • managers cannot know whether changed conditions invalidate the model.

Responses:

  • prefer interpretable model for high-impact decisions where performance difference is small;
  • use explainability tools and reason codes;
  • maintain model documentation, data lineage and logs;
  • independent validation and audit;
  • human oversight/appeal;
  • restrict autonomous decisions;
  • monitor drift and disparate outcomes.

26.4 Large-scale collection and surveillanceLink to this section

Problems include lack of genuine consent, secondary use beyond original purpose, re-identification of “anonymous” data, chilling effects on employees/customers, power imbalance, security risk and inference of sensitive traits.

Ask:

  • Is every field necessary?
  • Would individuals reasonably expect this use?
  • Could the objective be met with aggregated/anonymised data?
  • Is monitoring proportionate?
  • Who benefits and who bears risk?
  • Can individuals opt out without unfair penalty?

26.5 Ethical issues in employee analyticsLink to this section

Potential benefit: improve safety, workloads, training and wellbeing.

Risks: intrusive monitoring, inaccurate productivity scores, loss of trust, work intensification, discrimination, manipulation to improve measured behaviour and use outside the original purpose.

Recommendations: consult employees; collect minimum data; distinguish support from punishment; allow correction/appeal; avoid one-dimensional algorithmic targets; assess team/process causes; limit manager access; review behavioural consequences.

26.6 Ethical issues in customer analytics and dynamic pricingLink to this section

Personalisation can improve relevance and capacity use, but may:

  • exploit vulnerability or urgency;
  • discriminate through proxies;
  • charge different prices without understandable justification;
  • manipulate choices;
  • create filter bubbles;
  • use personal data beyond customer expectations.

Evaluate transparency, fairness, necessity, customer value, reputation and long-term trust—not profit alone.

26.7 ACCA professional ethics connectionLink to this section

Apply the fundamental principles:

  • Integrity: do not manipulate data/models or conceal adverse output.
  • Objectivity: resist management bias, conflicts and undue influence.
  • Professional competence and due care: understand limitations; use specialists; validate before advice.
  • Confidentiality: protect data and do not misuse it.
  • Professional behaviour: comply with law and avoid conduct discrediting the profession.

Threats may include self-interest (bonus depends on model result), self-review (designer validates own model), advocacy, familiarity and intimidation. Safeguards include independent validation, transparent assumptions, audit trail, escalation, ethical review and declining association with misleading analysis.

27. Role of the management accountant in data scienceLink to this section

The management accountant does not need to be the programmer. The strategic role is to bridge technical specialists and decision-makers by:

  • translating strategy into analytics questions and measurable outcomes;
  • defining CSFs, KPIs and decision constraints;
  • challenging data quality, assumptions and causality;
  • combining financial with operational/customer information;
  • assessing cost, benefit, risk and stakeholder impact;
  • interpreting output with commercial acumen;
  • communicating uncertainty clearly;
  • ensuring controls, governance and ethics;
  • measuring whether recommendations create sustainable value;
  • resisting automation bias and preserving accountability.

PART III — WHAT COULD BE EXAMINED?Link to this section

28. Probable Section D question patternsLink to this section

ACCA can combine topics in many ways; these are plausible patterns, not predictions. A question will be scenario-based and likely supported by exhibits such as a board proposal, dataset, consultant report, dashboard, regression/model output, email or system specification.

Pattern 1: New ERP/unified database proposalLink to this section

Possible requirements:

  • evaluate how the ERP would improve performance management;
  • assess data-silo problems;
  • evaluate implementation risks;
  • recommend controls and implementation approach.

High-value scenario clues: inconsistent divisional KPIs, duplicate entry, late consolidation, reconciliation differences, local autonomy, poor master data, weak training.

Pattern 2: Cloud migration/cyber incidentLink to this section

Possible requirements:

  • recommend suitable data sources;
  • explain the data-science process;
  • identify privacy and ethical problems.

  • classify examples as descriptive, diagnostic, predictive or prescriptive;
  • explain how each improves performance;
  • recommend an analytical sequence for a stated problem.

Do not only define. For each: identify data, resulting insight, decision/KPI, benefit and limitation.

Possible requirements:

  • interpret coefficients, R², ranges/distribution and trends;
  • assess the suitability of the model;
  • advise management on the forecast and action.

Possible requirements:

  • assess AI/ML use for churn, fraud, demand or maintenance;
  • evaluate model output and error rates;
  • recommend refinements;
  • explain model insight to the board.

  • challenge a claim that “the algorithm is objective”;

  • explain possible performance insight;
  • assess limitations and ethics.

Possible requirements:

  • evaluate the system for performance management;
  • recommend KPIs and controls;
  • discuss behavioural/implementation issues.

29. Cross-syllabus integrations you should expectLink to this section

Strategy, CSFs and KPIsLink to this section

Technology is a means, not an objective. Ask which mission/objective it supports. Convert analytical insight into leading and lagging KPIs.

Example: strategy is reliable delivery. Relevant outputs/KPIs include predicted late deliveries, on-time-in-full rate, average delay, complaint rate, repeat purchases and contribution lost.

Balanced ScorecardLink to this section

  • Financial: margin, cash conversion, system ROI and cost-to-serve.
  • Customer: retention, satisfaction, NPS, service response.
  • Internal process: cycle time, error/defect rate, downtime.
  • Learning and growth: digital skills, KMS usage, innovation and employee engagement.

Analytics can show links between perspectives, e.g. training → fewer errors → satisfaction → retention → profit. Test rather than assume the causal chain.

Budgeting and forecastingLink to this section

Predictive analytics can create rolling, driver-based forecasts and scenarios. Benefits are speed and responsiveness; risks include false precision, unstable historic relationships and managers losing understanding/ownership of assumptions.

Reward and behaviourLink to this section

Algorithmic KPIs can intensify “what gets measured gets done”. Risks: gaming, data manipulation, tunnel vision, short-termism, controllability problems and unfair rewards from inaccurate models. Use balanced measures, controllable factors, review/appeal and avoid sole reliance on one metric.

Performance improvementLink to this section

  • sensor data supports JIT but dependency increases disruption risk;
  • analytics can identify non-value activities for ABM/BPR;
  • machine learning can support Six Sigma defect analysis;
  • text analytics can convert voice of customer into quality improvement;
  • automation should follow process simplification, not preserve waste.

Services and not-for-profitsLink to this section

Use analytics to measure access, waiting time, quality, outcome and equity—not only cost. Data collection in healthcare, education and public services is particularly sensitive. A model may improve efficiency while reducing effectiveness or fair access.

SustainabilityLink to this section

IoT and analytics can measure energy, waste, emissions and supply-chain performance. Challenge completeness, boundaries, estimation assumptions and greenwashing. Processing and storage also have environmental costs.

30. Requirement verbs: what the marker expectsLink to this section

  • Explain: show how/why, not merely state.
  • Apply: use the scenario’s facts and organisation.
  • Analyse: break down evidence, calculate/interpret relationships and identify reasons/consequences.
  • Assess: weigh significance, strength and limitations before a judgement.
  • Evaluate: balanced advantages/disadvantages in the specific context, ending with a supported conclusion.
  • Advise/recommend: state what management should do, why, how and subject to which safeguards.
  • Identify: concise recognition may be enough, but scenario relevance still matters.

If the verb is “evaluate”, generic benefits alone cannot score highly. If “advise”, a list of risks without action is incomplete.

31. Section B time and mark strategyLink to this section

The full exam is 195 minutes for 100 marks: approximately 1.95 minutes per mark. A 25-mark question therefore deserves about 49 minutes, including reading and planning.

A practical allocation:

  1. 6–8 minutes: read requirements first, then exhibits; highlight role, audience, verbs and deliverables.
  2. 3–4 minutes: make a brief answer plan by requirement and mark allocation.
  3. 34–37 minutes: write focused points with evidence and judgement.
  4. 2–3 minutes: review that every requirement and professional skill is addressed.

Do not spend 15 minutes producing a perfect definition. Use mark allocation to judge breadth: a substantial 10-mark requirement normally needs several developed, distinct points.

32. A universal answer frameworkLink to this section

Use P–A–I–R for each paragraph:

  • P — Point: identify the relevant issue/benefit/risk.
  • A — Apply: use a fact from the exhibit.
  • I — Impact: explain effect on strategy, KPI, stakeholder, cost, risk or decision.
  • R — Recommend/Reach judgement: conclude, control, refinement or action.

Example:

The consultant’s training sample excludes cash customers (Point). They represent 35% of the retailer’s transactions and have different purchasing patterns (Apply). The demand model may therefore understate low-value products and produce stock-outs, reducing availability and customer satisfaction (Impact). Cash transactions should be included or a separate segment model developed and validated before rollout (Recommend).

One well-developed paragraph is normally more valuable than several unexplained buzzwords.

33. Professional skills marks in the Section D questionLink to this section

The 25 marks are expected to comprise 20 technical and 5 professional-skills marks. At least two skills from analysis and evaluation, scepticism and commercial acumen will be examined.

33.1 Analysis and evaluationLink to this section

Demonstrate by:

  • selecting relevant exhibit data rather than copying it all;
  • calculating/interpreting trends, proportions or model metrics;
  • linking cause, consequence and strategic objective;
  • weighing benefits against cost/risk;
  • prioritising material issues;
  • reaching a reasoned judgement.

Weak: “AI increases accuracy.”

Strong: “Although model accuracy is 92%, only 2% of transactions are fraudulent, so accuracy may mainly reflect correctly classifying non-fraud. Recall, false-positive cost and loss prevented are more relevant before replacing manual review.”

33.2 ScepticismLink to this section

Demonstrate by:

  • challenging unsupported management/consultant claims;
  • distinguishing correlation from causality;
  • questioning source, completeness, assumptions and incentives;
  • identifying contradictory evidence;
  • requesting further evidence;
  • refining, rather than automatically accepting/rejecting, a proposal.

Useful questions:

  • Who produced this output and what incentives do they have?
  • What data or stakeholder is missing?
  • Is the comparison like-for-like?
  • Does the metric measure the objective or only convenient activity?
  • What alternative explanation exists?
  • How was the model validated?

33.3 Commercial acumenLink to this section

Demonstrate by:

  • understanding how the organisation creates value;
  • balancing full cost, benefit, risk and implementation feasibility;
  • considering customers, competitors, capacity, culture and regulation;
  • recommending prioritised and practical actions;
  • protecting long-term trust rather than chasing a short-term metric.

Strong recommendations include pilot, owner, timescale, KPI and review threshold.

33.4 CommunicationLink to this section

Although the Section B skills are selected from the three above, communicate professionally throughout:

  • follow the required format and audience;
  • use clear headings matching requirements;
  • concise paragraphs and relevant tables where useful;
  • explain technical output in plain language;
  • avoid unexplained abbreviations;
  • state a conclusion.

34. Model answer plans for common requirementsLink to this section

34.1 “Evaluate the proposed use of AI to forecast demand”Link to this section

Plan:

  1. Strategic need: reduce stock-outs/waste and improve margins/service.
  2. Benefits: variables/volume, speed, non-linear patterns, granular forecasts, scenarios.
  3. Data: sales, price, promotions, weather, events, competitor activity; quality and representativeness.
  4. Model: validation, baseline comparison, error measures, uncertainty, overfitting/drift.
  5. Operations: integration with ordering, manager override, supplier/capacity constraints.
  6. Financial case: contribution saved less implementation/lifecycle cost.
  7. Ethics/security where customer-level data used.
  8. Recommendation: pilot high-volume category, compare with current forecast, track MAE/stock-outs/waste, retain review.

34.2 “Assess the output and recommend refinements”Link to this section

Plan:

  1. Restate purpose and success metric.
  2. Interpret output, including direction, magnitude and uncertainty.
  3. Compare performance with baseline and business tolerance.
  4. Identify segment/time weaknesses and false error costs.
  5. Challenge causality, data/sample and assumptions.
  6. Assess fairness/explainability/actionability.
  7. Refine data, model, threshold, segmentation, controls or pilot.
  8. Establish monitoring/retraining.

34.3 “Advise on ethical issues”Link to this section

Plan:

  1. Data purpose/necessity and affected stakeholders.
  2. Consent/control and reasonable expectation.
  3. Transparency and ability to understand/challenge.
  4. Privacy, confidentiality, security and retention.
  5. Bias, representativeness, proxies and feedback loops.
  6. Harm/outcomes and proportionality.
  7. Accountability and human oversight.
  8. Safeguards and recommendation.
  9. Link to integrity, objectivity, competence, confidentiality and professional behaviour.

34.4 “Evaluate ERP for performance management”Link to this section

Plan:

  1. Current silo/inconsistency problem from scenario.
  2. Single data source, automation, timeliness, process integration and drill-down.
  3. Better decisions/KPIs tied to strategy.
  4. Cost, disruption, migration, standardisation, skills, resistance and cyber risk.
  5. Whether standard system suits operations.
  6. Business case and staged implementation.
  7. Data governance, training, access, reconciliation and post-implementation KPIs.

34.5 “Analyse regression output and advise management”Link to this section

Plan:

  1. Define dependent/independent variables and business purpose.
  2. Interpret signs and coefficients with units.
  3. Explain R²/other output without overclaiming.
  4. Discuss trend, spread, outliers and sample.
  5. Challenge causality, omitted variables, extrapolation and structural change.
  6. Explain forecast uncertainty and operational error costs.
  7. Recommend validation, further variables/data, scenarios and controlled action.

35. Mini practice case with model pointsLink to this section

ScenarioLink to this section

FreshBox delivers subscription meal kits. Churn has increased from 5% to 9%. A consultant has built a black-box model using customer age, postcode, order history, complaints, app clicks and recorded customer-service calls. It claims 94% accuracy and recommends automatically offering a 30% discount to customers predicted to leave. The model was trained on active customers who paid by card during the last 12 months; cash and recently cancelled customers were excluded. Managers propose recording all calls without a specific notification because the terms and conditions permit “service monitoring”.

Requirement A: Evaluate the model and proposed useLink to this section

Strong points:

  • A churn model is relevant to retention and recurring revenue, but success should be incremental contribution retained, not accuracy alone.
  • 94% accuracy may be misleading if most customers do not churn; require confusion matrix, recall, precision and segment error rates.
  • Excluding cancelled customers is severe survivorship/selection bias because these are the clearest churn outcomes.
  • Excluding cash customers means the model may not represent the whole customer base.
  • Twelve months may not capture seasonality or changing economic conditions.
  • Postcode may proxy income, ethnicity or other sensitive characteristics and cause unfair treatment.
  • App clicks may be useful leading indicators, but could reflect device/access differences rather than dissatisfaction.
  • A black box limits explanation, audit and challenge; seek reason codes or a simpler model.
  • A universal 30% discount may retain unprofitable customers, reduce margin and train customers to threaten cancellation.
  • Target high-risk and high-value customers with tailored service recovery; solve root causes such as complaints.
  • Test against a control group and compare contribution, retention, discount cost and customer fairness.
  • Retain human approval for unusual/high-value cases and monitor drift.

Requirement B: Advise on ethical issuesLink to this section

Strong points:

  • Broad legal wording does not ensure customers reasonably understand AI analysis of calls; provide specific, accessible notice.
  • Recording and emotional inference may be disproportionate; collect only necessary data.
  • Protect recordings with encryption, restricted access, short retention and deletion.
  • Assess whether call data can be anonymised or converted to limited themes.
  • Audit model outcomes and error rates across groups; postcode and age can produce discrimination.
  • Explain significant decisions and allow customers to contest/correct data.
  • Board remains accountable; consultant/model cannot bear ethical responsibility.
  • Evaluate trust and reputation: covert-feeling monitoring may worsen churn.

Suggested conclusionLink to this section

Do not deploy automatic discounts in the current form. Rebuild/validate using representative outcomes, use a transparent value-based intervention policy, run a controlled pilot and introduce clear privacy, fairness and human-review safeguards.

36. Common mistakes that lose marksLink to this section

  1. Writing definitions with no scenario application.
  2. Treating technology as automatically beneficial.
  3. Listing every advantage/disadvantage regardless of the requirement.
  4. Repeating exhibit facts without explaining significance.
  5. Confusing data volume with data value.
  6. Claiming correlation proves causation.
  7. Saying a high R² or accuracy proves a model is reliable.
  8. Ignoring base rates and false-positive/false-negative costs.
  9. Recommending more data without purpose, consent or cost.
  10. Giving “improve security” as a control without specificity.
  11. Discussing ethics only as legal compliance.
  12. Assuming anonymisation always prevents identification.
  13. Saying remove sensitive variables and assuming bias disappears.
  14. Failing to give a conclusion or recommendation.
  15. Ignoring implementation, people and culture.
  16. Ignoring financial value and commercial feasibility.
  17. Using generic AI buzzwords not found in the scenario.
  18. Spending too long on one requirement.
  19. Missing the required report/memo/advice format.
  20. Not addressing professional skills explicitly through the quality of analysis.
